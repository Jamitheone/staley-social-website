Blueprint · Architecture

Stop prompting. Start running loops.

The single biggest shift from "I use AI" to "AI runs things for me" is this: the unit of work stops being a prompt and becomes a loop. A prompt happens once, when you're sitting there. A loop runs on its own trigger, forever, and reports to you. My whole system is a stack of these, not a person typing at a chat box.

The contract every loop has to sign

If you can't fill in all six of these, you don't have a loop, you have a wish. Write them down before you build anything:

  1. Trigger — time, event, or condition that starts it.
  2. Action — the work it does.
  3. Oracle — the outside number that proves it worked. Not a vibe. Replies received, dollars in, errors down.
  4. Memory — what persists between runs so it never repeats itself.
  5. Output — what it delivers, and where (a text, a file, a posted thing).
  6. Exit — when it stops or hands back to you.

The move most people miss: a watcher on the watcher

Once you have loops running while you sleep, you need something checking that they actually ran. A loop that silently dies looks identical to a quiet day. So every important loop gets a second, dumb loop watching its oracle: if the number a loop is supposed to move hasn't moved, or its heartbeat file is stale, the watcher texts me. Cheap insurance against a machine that's quietly doing nothing.

Copy this

Take one thing you check manually every day. Write its six-part contract. Build it, schedule it, then build a two-line watcher that texts you only if its oracle goes flat. That's the whole pattern the rest of the system is made of.

Blueprint · Autonomy

How to go hands-off without blowing yourself up

Everyone wants "set it and forget it." The reason most people can't is they treat autonomy as all-or-nothing. It isn't. The trick is a hard line drawn in exactly one place.

Automate everything up to the irreversible edge

Reading, researching, drafting, building, scheduling, logging, analyzing: automate all of it, fully, no human. The only things that pause for a yes are the four irreversible, outward-facing verbs: send, post, spend, delete. Everything else runs itself. That single rule lets you automate aggressively without waking up to a disaster.

The queue is the checkpoint

Don't make the human approval a blocking pop-up you have to babysit. Make it a queue. The system does all the work, then drops the one risky step into a list with everything already prepared: the draft written, the numbers pulled, the reasoning shown. You approve or reject in a tap, from your phone, whenever. Approved items fire. The work was never the bottleneck, only the final yes was, so that's the only thing that waits for you.

Then audit the audit

The last level: something that grades whether your own approvals and your system's self-checks are actually catching problems, not just rubber-stamping. A machine that says "done" is worthless without a second machine that proves it. Every "done" needs an oracle or a real test behind it, and something spot-checking that those checks aren't lying.

Copy this

Go through your automations and label every step as reversible or irreversible. Reversible steps: cut the human out entirely. Irreversible steps: route them into one approval queue with the work pre-done. You'll be shocked how little actually needs you.

Blueprint · Compounding

The flywheel: never pay for the same lesson twice

This is the mechanism that makes my system get smarter every single week without me sitting down to "improve" it. It's a rule, and it runs on every wall I hit.

The rule

When you hit a wall, a broken tool, a blocked API, a fix that took an hour to find, you do not just solve it and move on. You solve it, then you capture it: as a reusable skill if it's a technique, or as a one-line memory with a trace back to where you learned it if it's a gotcha. Next time that wall shows up, the fix is already in the system. You paid the research cost once, globally, forever.

Make the capture automatic, too

Relying on yourself to remember to write the lesson down is a weak link. So the capture itself is a loop: overnight, a job reads the day's work, pulls out the mistakes and the corrections, distills them with a local model, and files them into the knowledge base. I wake up to a system that already learned from yesterday. The human capturing lessons is a nice-to-have, the automatic path has no human in it.

Copy this

Start a single file called lessons.md. Every time you and your AI fight through a wall, add one line: what broke, what fixed it, dated. Tell your AI to read it before similar tasks. That one file, growing, is worth more than any course, including this one.

Blueprint · Reliability

Make your agents survive the real world

A demo works once, on your machine, while you watch. A system runs unattended for months across sleep, network drops, and updates. The gap between those two is where every "it just stopped working" lives. Here's what actually closes it.

  • Degrade gracefully, never crash. When a dependency is down, an agent should do the part it still can and report what it skipped, not die with an error nobody sees. A dead agent and a busy one look the same until you check.
  • Guard the network. Anything that reaches the internet checks it has a connection first, and backs off and retries instead of failing on the first blip. Most "random failures" are just a wifi hiccup the code didn't expect.
  • Know your single point of failure. If ten loops all depend on one account, one key, one service, then that one thing going down takes down all ten at once. Find that chokepoint and make each loop survive it losing, even if it's just "skip today and text me."
  • Schedule it correctly. There's a right tool for "every morning" and a different right tool for "every 30 seconds." Using the wrong one is why a job silently never fires. Match the scheduler to the cadence.
Copy this

Take one loop you rely on. Ask: what happens if the internet's down when it fires? If the answer is "it crashes and I never know," you have a fix to make before you trust it unattended.

Blueprint · Efficiency

API first, browser last

When you want your AI to do something on a website, post, change a setting, pull data, the obvious move is to have it drive a browser: click, type, read the screen. It's also usually the wrong first move. Browser automation is slow, fragile, burns way more compute, and trips bot-detection that fails you anyway.

The order of operations

  1. Check for an official API first. Search "<the service> API <the thing you want>." Most real tools have one. An API call is a fraction of the cost, doesn't break when they restyle a button, and doesn't look like a bot.
  2. Only if there's genuinely no API do you fall back to driving a browser, and you treat it as the fragile path it is.

This ties into a bigger constraint I hold as a hard rule, not a nice-to-have: efficiency is part of quality. Wasted tokens, wasted API calls, wasted compute are a defect. Reuse what you've built, pick the cheap correct path, and never trade the result quality to save money, find the way that's both.

Copy this

Next time you're about to have your AI "go to the website and do X," stop and spend two minutes searching for that service's API. Nine times out of ten you'll find a path that's faster, cheaper, and doesn't break next week.

Blueprint · Comms

An assistant that answers in your voice

The highest-leverage loop I run reads my incoming messages, understands them, and replies the way I actually talk, so a real person gets a real answer fast even when I'm heads-down or asleep. Getting this right is more about restraint than cleverness.

What makes it not feel like a bot

  • Voice is mined, not guessed. Feed it your real past messages and let it learn your register, punctuation, and rhythm. A generic "professional" tone is the tell. Yours isn't generic.
  • Match the register to the person. How you text a client is not how you text a friend. The system reads who it's talking to and shifts, tight and clear for business, loose for people who know you.
  • Same checkpoint as everything else. It can read and draft freely. Whether a given reply auto-sends or waits for your tap depends on the stakes, and anything sensitive, or to someone who thinks they're talking to you, gets disclosed or held for a human. Never let a machine impersonate you into a real decision.
Copy this

Before you automate any reply, write your own "voice rules" file: the words you never use, your punctuation habits, how blunt you are. It's the difference between an assistant people like hearing from and one they can smell a mile away.
